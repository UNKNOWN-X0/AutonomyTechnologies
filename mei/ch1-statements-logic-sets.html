Statements, Logic, and Sets

Mathematics for Engineers I · Module 62765 · Chapter 1

Mathematical arguments use a small grammar of statements, logical connectives, and sets. This chapter builds that grammar: first decide when statements are true, then describe and combine collections of objects precisely.

1.1 Propositional Logic

Logic gives precise rules for combining claims. It lets you test arguments, simplify conditions, and express properties that will reappear throughout mathematics.

A statement has a truth value

A statement is a sentence that is either true or false; its truth value is 1 or 0, respectively. “9 > 8” is a statement. “Friday the 13th is unlucky” is not a statement here because it does not have a definite mathematical truth value. A claim may still be a statement even if its truth is not currently known.

Logical connectives¬AA ∧ BA ∨ BA ⇒ BA ⇔ BTruth tables define the meaning of each connective. The notes use 1 for true and 0 for false; ∨ is inclusive “or”.
FormMeaningTrue when
¬Anot AA is false
A ∧ BA and Bboth A and B are true
A ∨ BA or Bat least one is true; both may be true
A ⇒ BA implies BA is false or B is true
A ⇔ BA iff BA and B have the same truth value

The implication A ⇒ B is false in exactly one case: A is true and B is false. This is why a false premise makes the implication true under the truth-table definition; it does not claim that the conclusion follows from a false premise in ordinary causal language.

Worked example · build a truth table

Test whether A ⇒ B has the same truth values as ¬A ∨ B.

  1. List all input pairs: (1,1), (1,0), (0,1), (0,0).
  2. Evaluate ¬A ∨ B: 1, 0, 1, 1.
  3. Evaluate A ⇒ B: 1, 0, 1, 1.
  4. The columns match in every row, so the two forms are equivalent. §1.1

Tautologies and logical rules

A tautology is true for every assignment of truth values. Two forms are logically equivalent when their truth values agree in every row. A truth table is a direct proof for expressions with a small number of variables.

Rules to recognise and prove with truth tables¬¬A ⇔ AA ∧ B ⇔ B ∧ AA ∨ B ⇔ B ∨ AA ∧ (B ∧ C) ⇔ (A ∧ B) ∧ CA ∨ (B ∨ C) ⇔ (A ∨ B) ∨ CA ∧ (B ∨ C) ⇔ (A ∧ B) ∨ (A ∧ C)A ∨ (B ∧ C) ⇔ (A ∨ B) ∧ (A ∨ C)¬(A ∧ B) ⇔ ¬A ∨ ¬B¬(A ∨ B) ⇔ ¬A ∧ ¬BThese include double negation, commutativity, associativity, distributivity, and De Morgan’s laws (Theorem 1.4, notes p. 4).

Trap: the converse B ⇒ A is not generally equivalent to A ⇒ B. “If it rains, the street is wet” does not imply “if the street is wet, it rained.”

Predicates and quantifiers

A propositional form P(x) depends on variables; assigning values turns it into a statement. A quantifier specifies how many values in a domain must satisfy it.

Quantifiers∀x P(x): P(x) holds for every x in the domain∃x P(x): P(x) holds for at least one x∃!x P(x): P(x) holds for exactly one xThe domain matters. Quantifier order matters too: ∃D ∀P R(D,P) is not the same as ∀P ∃D R(D,P).

Negation changes “every” to “there exists at least one counterexample”, and changes “there exists” to “none exist”.

Negating quantified statements¬(∀x P(x)) ⇔ ∃x ¬P(x)¬(∃x P(x)) ⇔ ∀x ¬P(x)Negate the predicate after swapping the quantifier.

Worked example · negate a quantified claim

Negate: “For every real number x, x² ≥ 0.”

  1. Swap ∀ for ∃.
  2. Negate the predicate x² ≥ 0 to x² < 0.
  3. The negation is “There exists a real number x with x² < 0.” It is false, so the original claim is true. §1.1

Memory hook: to disprove a universal claim, give one counterexample. To prove an existential claim, give one witness.

Why the grammar matters

The notes connect propositional logic to digital circuits and satisfiability problems. A circuit implements a truth table; a SAT solver asks whether some assignment makes a propositional formula true. These are applications of the truth rules, not extra operators.

Try it: truth-table experiments and quantifier experiments.

Recall check · answer without looking

  1. Which row makes A ⇒ B false?
  2. How can you prove two forms equivalent with a truth table?
  3. What is the negation of ∀x P(x)?
Check these answers
  1. A=1, B=0. §1.1
  2. Show that their output columns agree for every assignment. §1.1
  3. ∃x ¬P(x): at least one value fails the predicate. §1.1

1.2 Set Theory

Sets let us collect objects by a precise membership rule. Logical connectives reappear here as operations on membership conditions.

Membership, descriptions, and inclusion

A set is a well-defined collection of distinct objects. Write x ∈ A when x is a member and x ∉ A otherwise. A set may be given by listing its elements, such as {1,2,3}, or by a property, such as {x ∈ ℝ : x > 0}. Order and repetition do not change a set.

Subset and equalityA ⊂ B ⇔ every x ∈ A also belongs to BA = B ⇔ A ⊂ B and B ⊂ AA ⊊ B ⇔ A ⊂ B and some element of B is not in AThe notes use ⊂ for subset and ⊊ for proper subset. Some authors use ⊆ for subset.

The empty set ∅ has no elements and is a subset of every set. Standard number sets are ℕ (positive natural numbers in these notes), ℤ (integers), ℚ (rationals), ℝ (reals), and ℂ (complex numbers).

Intervals, power sets, and size

Intervals describe parts of the real line. Round endpoints are excluded and square endpoints included: (a,b), [a,b), (a,b], [a,b]. Unbounded intervals include (a,∞) and (−∞,b]; the notes also use ℝ=(−∞,∞), ℝ+=(0,∞), and ℝ≥0=[0,∞). The power set 𝒫(X) contains every subset of X. For finite X, cardinality |X| counts its elements, and |𝒫(X)| = 2|X|.

Worked example · enumerate a power set

Let X={a,b}. List 𝒫(X) and its size.

  1. Include the empty subset: ∅.
  2. Include the two one-element subsets: {a}, {b}.
  3. Include the whole set: {a,b}. Therefore 𝒫(X)={∅,{a},{b},{a,b}} and |𝒫(X)|=4=2². §1.2

Set operations

Each operation is easiest to understand by asking when an arbitrary element x belongs to the result. Sets with empty intersection are called disjoint.

Operations and membership testsx ∈ A ∪ B ⇔ (x ∈ A) ∨ (x ∈ B)x ∈ A ∩ B ⇔ (x ∈ A) ∧ (x ∈ B)x ∈ A \ B ⇔ (x ∈ A) ∧ (x ∉ B)A × B = {(a,b): a ∈ A and b ∈ B}Aᶜ = X \ A, where X is the fixed non-empty universal setPairs are ordered: (a,b) is generally different from (b,a). A complement is defined relative to X.

Union collects elements in either set; intersection keeps elements in both; difference keeps elements in A but removes those in B. The Cartesian product contains ordered pairs, not sets of the factors.

Operations on n sets⋃k=1nAk contains x when x belongs to at least one Ak⋂k=1nAk contains x when x belongs to every Ak∏k=1nAk = A1×⋯×An, the ordered n-tuples (x1,…,xn) with xk∈AkIn particular, ℝ2=ℝ×ℝ is the plane and ℝ3=ℝ×ℝ×ℝ is three-dimensional space.

Worked example · combine operations

Let A={1,2,3} and B={3,4,5}. Find A∪B, A∩B, and A\B.

  1. Membership in at least one gives A∪B={1,2,3,4,5}.
  2. Membership in both gives A∩B={3}, so the sets are not disjoint.
  3. Members of A that are not in B give A\B={1,2}. §1.2

Set algebra and De Morgan’s laws

Set identities can be proved by translating both sides into membership conditions. For example, x ∈ (A∩B)ᶜ exactly when x∉A∩B; this means it is not true that x is in both sets, or equivalently x∉A or x∉B. Thus x∈Aᶜ∪Bᶜ, proving (A∩B)ᶜ=Aᶜ∪Bᶜ.

Set-algebra rules (Theorem 1.13)(Aᶜ)ᶜ=AA∩B=B∩AA∪B=B∪AA∩(B∩C)=(A∩B)∩CA∪(B∪C)=(A∪B)∪CA∩(B∪C)=(A∩B)∪(A∩C)A∪(B∩C)=(A∪B)∩(A∪C)(A∩B)ᶜ=Aᶜ∪Bᶜ(A∪B)ᶜ=Aᶜ∩BᶜComplements use the same fixed universal set X.

Trap: A\B is not generally B\A. Check the direction by testing membership: the first set supplies candidates; the second removes them.

Explain it

Answer each “why” in your own words before checking the model response.

  1. Why is A⇒B true when A is false under the truth-table definition?
  2. Why does matching truth-table columns prove equivalence?
  3. Why can one counterexample disprove a universal statement?
  4. Why does quantifier order change the meaning of a claim about dogs and people?
  5. Why must the universal set be fixed to define a complement?
  6. Why is A\B different from B\A?
  7. Why are ordered pairs in A×B generally not the same as those in B×A?
  8. Why can a set identity be proved by comparing membership conditions?
Show model answers
  1. The defined truth condition is “A is false or B is true.” Only a true premise paired with a false conclusion violates it. §1.1
  2. Equivalence means same output for every possible input; the table lists every input assignment. §1.1
  3. A universal claim says every domain element works, so one failing element is enough to make it false. §1.1
  4. ∃D∀P can use one dog for all people; ∀P∃D allows a different dog for each person. §1.1
  5. “Outside A” means outside A but still within the chosen universe; changing the universe changes the complement. §1.2
  6. A\B starts from A and removes elements of B; reversing the order changes both the candidates and what is removed. §1.2
  7. The first coordinate must come from A and the second from B, so swapping the factors swaps coordinate roles. §1.2
  8. Two sets are equal when they contain exactly the same elements, so membership of an arbitrary x captures equality. §1.2

Exam-style problems

Set a timer for about 20 minutes per problem. Write the reasoning, not just the final values.

1 · Logic for a sensor check

Three sensors report truth values A, B, and C. A majority vote is represented by V=(A∧B)∨(A∧C)∨(B∧C).

  1. Make a truth table for V and identify the rows where V=1.
  2. Show that swapping any two sensor labels leaves V unchanged, using commutativity of ∧ and ∨.
  3. Negate the claim “For every sensor configuration with at most one report equal to 1, the majority output is 0.” State the form of a counterexample.
Show the worked answer
  1. V=1 when at least two reports are 1: rows 111, 110, 101, 011. It is 0 for 100, 010, 001, 000. §1.1
  2. For example, swapping A and B changes (A∧B) to (B∧A), which is equal by commutativity; it swaps the other two terms as well. The full disjunction is unchanged, and the same argument applies to any pair. §1.1
  3. ∃ a configuration with at most one 1 but V=1. This cannot happen for the majority rule, so the original claim is true. §1.1

2 · Sets of admissible inputs

Let the universal set be X={1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8}. Let A be the even elements of X, B={1,2,3,4}, and C={3,4,5,6}.

  1. List A, A∩B, and A∪B.
  2. Find (B∪C)ᶜ and Bᶜ∩Cᶜ relative to X, and use the results to check a De Morgan identity.
  3. Let D={red, blue}. List A×D and give its cardinality.
Show the worked answer
  1. A={2,4,6,8}; A∩B={2,4}; A∪B={1,2,3,4,6,8}. §1.2
  2. B∪C={1,2,3,4,5,6}, so (B∪C)ᶜ={7,8}. Also Bᶜ={5,6,7,8} and Cᶜ={1,2,7,8}; their intersection is {7,8}. Hence (B∪C)ᶜ=Bᶜ∩Cᶜ. §1.2
  3. A×D={(2,red),(2,blue),(4,red),(4,blue),(6,red),(6,blue),(8,red),(8,blue)}; it has 8 ordered pairs. §1.2

Slips in the notes

One statement in the lecture notes needs an edge-case qualification.

  1. Printed page 9: the notes say that A×B is neither contained in A nor in B. If either factor is empty, then A×B=∅, and ∅ is a subset of both factors. The definition of the Cartesian product remains correct; the remark about containment is too broad for empty factors.
