Stationary Currents

Electrical Engineering I · Module 92315 · Chapter 2

A potential difference drives charge through a conductor. This chapter connects that motion to current density, material conductivity, interfaces, resistance, and the energy that becomes heat.

Start from memory

Try these six prompts before you read. The aim is to find gaps, not to get them all right.

  1. What does electric current measure, and what is its SI unit?
  2. How are current density, charge density, and carrier velocity related?
  3. What must the net current through a closed surface be in a stationary situation?
  4. Why do electrons drift opposite to the electric field while conventional current points with it?
  5. At an interface between conductors, which components of J and E stay continuous?
  6. How are electrical power and energy related for a resistor?
Check the short answers
  1. Current is charge crossing a conductor section per unit time; its SI unit is the ampere (A).
  2. J = ϱVv, where ϱV is volume charge density and v is carrier velocity.
  3. Zero: as much charge enters the closed surface as leaves it, so ∮A J · dA = 0.
  4. Electrons are negative, so the electric force is opposite E; conventional current is defined in the direction positive charge would move.
  5. The normal component Jn and tangential component Et are continuous.
  6. Power is energy transferred per time; for constant DC values in a resistor, P = U I and W = P T.

2.1.1 Electric current

Current gives one number for the total charge flow across a chosen section. It is the circuit quantity that links a moving charge to measurable transport.

In plain words: imagine counting the net charge that passes a cut through a wire. Current is how quickly that count changes. Opposite-moving charges subtract from one another in the net flow.

Instantaneous currentI = dQdtRead it as: “current is the rate at which charge crosses the section”.For a short interval, I ≈ ΔQ/Δt. Unit: A = C/s. Positive direction is conventional current, the direction positive charge would move.

When a conducting path joins two electrodes at different potentials, the field drives carriers. The flow continues while the source maintains that potential difference; without a source, the finite charge separation relaxes and the electrode potentials approach one another. In a metal, electrons move opposite the conventional current direction.

Worked example

A net charge of 3.6 C crosses a wire section in 9.0 s at a steady rate. Find the current.

  1. Use I = ΔQ/Δt because the rate is steady.
  2. I = 3.6 C / 9.0 s = 0.40 A.
  3. Check: 0.40 C passes each second, so 9.0 s carries 3.6 C.

Trap: current is not the amount of charge stored in the wire. It is the rate of net charge crossing a section.

Near miss: 2 C moving right and 2 C moving left in the same interval do not give 4 C of net transport through a cut; their signed contributions cancel.

2.1.2 Electric current density

Total current hides where carriers move and how they cross a surface. Current density keeps the local direction and concentration of the flow.

In plain words: current density is a map of charge flow. Its arrow points along conventional current, and its size tells you the current passing through each unit of area perpendicular to that arrow.

Current density from moving chargeJ(P) = ϱV(P) v(P)Read it as: “at P, current density equals volume charge density times carrier velocity”.ϱV is signed charge per volume in C/m3; v is the velocity of that signed charge, so J points with conventional current. Unit: A/m2.
Current through a surfaceI = ∬A J · dARead it as: “current through A is the surface integral of current density dotted with the oriented area element”.The area vector points along the chosen surface normal. For uniform J meeting a flat surface at angle α to its normal, I = |J|A cos α. Unit: A.

The notes define the microscopic current density using the signed volume charge density. For electrons, both ϱV and their drift velocity are negative relative to conventional current, so their product points in the conventional current direction. For a uniform perpendicular flow, the surface integral reduces to I = J A.

Worked example

A cylindrical wire of diameter 1.0 mm carries 5.0 A uniformly. Find its current density and the electron drift speed if the free-electron density is 8.5 × 1028 m−3.

  1. A = π(0.50 mm)2 = 0.785 mm2 = 7.85 × 10−7 m2.
  2. J = I/A = 5.0 / (7.85 × 10−7) = 6.37 × 106 A/m2.
  3. For electron carriers, |J| = nee|ve|. Thus |ve| = 6.37 × 106 / (8.5 × 1028 × 1.602 × 10−19) = 4.68 × 10−4 m/s, opposite J.
  4. Check: the drift speed is small because an enormous number of electrons share the current.

Trap: use the area perpendicular to the current for I = J A. For an oblique surface, the dot product supplies the cosine.

Near miss: the speed of an individual electron is not the drift speed. Thermal motion is rapid in random directions; drift is the much smaller average velocity.

  2. Why does electron current density point opposite the electron velocity?
Answers
  1. The component of J normal to the oriented surface; tangential flow does not cross it.
  2. Electron charge density is negative, so ϱVv points opposite the electron velocity.

2.1.3 Definition of stationary currents

A stationary current does not pile charge up at any point. That conservation condition is what makes currents match across a conducting path and at an interface.

In plain words: “stationary” means the flow pattern at each place stays the same over time. Charge can keep moving, but the amount inside any closed boundary does not grow or shrink.

Stationary current∂I∂t = 0,   ∂J∂t = 0Read it as: “current and current density have no time change”.The current and current density are constant in time. They may still vary from place to place.
Zero net current through a closed surface∯A J · dA = 0Read it as: “the outward current integrated over a closed surface is zero”.Use the outward normal. Inflow and outflow have opposite signs. Unit: A.

The second relation expresses the continuity principle for the stationary case: carriers are not created or destroyed inside the surface, and the enclosed charge stays constant. It does not say that the current through every open surface is zero; an open cross-section can carry a nonzero current.

Trap: zero net current through a closed surface does not mean zero current everywhere. A wire can pass through the surface: current enters on one side and leaves on the other.

Near miss: constant current through a wire is stationary even though carriers move. Stationary describes time dependence, not whether the charges are motionless.

Try it in the Chapter 2 lab: 2.1 Current flow

Recall check

  1. Can stationary J vary with position?
  2. What does the closed-surface integral say about inward and outward current?
Answers
  1. Yes. It is constant in time at each position, but its value can differ from place to place.
  2. The signed total is zero: total inflow equals total outflow.

2.2.1 Charge movements in conductors

The motion of carriers explains why a field inside a current-carrying wire is nonzero and how material properties enter Ohm’s law.

In plain words: in a metal, some outer electrons can move through the lattice. Without an applied field, their random thermal motion averages to no current. A field adds a small preferred drift to that random motion.

Electron drift velocityve = −μeERead it as: “electron drift velocity is minus the mobility times electric field”.μe > 0 is electron mobility, dependent on material and temperature. Unit: m2/(V s). The minus sign means electron drift is opposite E.

Electrons repeatedly collide with the lattice. The field accelerates them between collisions, while scattering prevents their speed from increasing without bound. The resulting average drift speed is proportional to field strength in the model used by these notes. Conventional current still points along E.

Worked example

For μe = 4.12 × 10−3 m2/(V s) and E = 0.20 V/m, find the electron drift velocity.

  1. Keep the direction in the signed relation: ve = −μeE.
  2. ve = −(4.12 × 10−3)(0.20) = −8.24 × 10−4 m/s along the field axis.
  3. Check: increasing positive field makes the electron drift more negative; the direction matches a negative carrier.

Trap: do not give electron drift and conventional current the same direction. The signed carrier density in J = ϱVv accounts for the reversal.

Near miss: random thermal motion by itself is not a directed current: its average velocity is zero. The field creates the nonzero drift average.

Try it in the Chapter 2 lab: 2.2 Drift and conductivity

Recall check

  1. What sets the sign between ve and E?
  2. Why is the average electron velocity zero before a field is applied?
Answers
  1. Electrons are negatively charged, so their electric force and drift are opposite the field.
  2. The thermal motion is disordered with equal likelihood in every direction, so the macroscopic mean cancels.

2.2.2 Ohm’s law in differential form

Local Ohm’s law describes how current density responds to electric field in a material.

In plain words: conductivity tells how much current a material carries for a given field; high κ needs less E for the same J.

Local Ohm’s lawJ(P) = κE(P) = E(P)ρRRead it as: “at P, current density is conductivity times electric field, or field divided by resistivity”.κ = 1/ρR; units: S/m, Ω m, A/m2, V/m respectively.
Conductivity from carrier motionκ = neeμeRead it as: “conductivity equals free-electron density times charge magnitude times mobility”.ne is in m−3; e is in C.
Linear temperature approximationρR(T) = ρR,293 K[1 + α(T − 293 K)]κ(T) = κ293 K1 + α(T − 293 K)Read it as: “resistivity changes linearly from its 293 kelvin value; conductivity is its reciprocal”.α is in K−1; this approximation is near 293 K. ΔT may be in K or °C. α depends on the material.

Worked example

Given κ = 4.0 × 107 S/m and J = 8.0 × 105 A/m2, find E and ρR.

  1. Rearrange local Ohm’s law: E = J/κ = (8.0 × 105)/(4.0 × 107) = 2.0 × 10−2 V/m.
  2. ρR = 1/κ = 2.5 × 10−8 Ω m.
  3. Check: κE = 4.0 × 107 × 2.0 × 10−2 = 8.0 × 105 A/m2.

Trap: resistivity ρR and volume charge density ϱV are different quantities with different units.

Near miss: if current density doubles in the same ohmic material at the same temperature, the field doubles too; the relation is linear, not inverse.

Try it in the Chapter 2 lab: 2.2 Drift and conductivity

Recall check

  1. How are κ and ρR related?
  2. For a fixed metal, what does positive α predict when temperature rises?
Answers
  1. They are reciprocals: κ = 1/ρR.
  2. Resistivity rises and conductivity falls in the linear approximation.

2.2.3 Ohm’s law in integral form

Most wire problems need only one voltage, one current, and a resistance. Integrating the local relation gives that simpler model when the conductor is uniform.

In plain words: resistance combines a material’s opposition to current with the path’s shape. A long, thin wire has more resistance; a short, thick wire made of the same material has less.

Resistance of a uniform cylindrical conductorR = ρRlA = lκARead it as: “resistance is resistivity times length divided by cross-sectional area”.Assumes a homogeneous material and uniform current over the cross-section. Unit: Ω.
Integral Ohm’s lawU = R I,   I = UR = G U,   G = 1RVoltage U and current I use the same positive direction convention here. Units: U in V, I in A, R in Ω, conductance G in S = 1/Ω.

The derivation uses J = I/A and E = J/κ along a uniform length l. The line integral of E gives the voltage drop, so U = I l/(κA) and the coefficient of I is the resistance. For temperature-dependent resistivity, use the value at the conductor’s operating temperature.

Worked example

A copper wire is 10 m long, has area 0.78 mm2, and uses ρR,293 K = 0.0178 Ω mm2/m. Find its resistance and voltage drop at 5.0 A, ignoring heating.

  1. R = 0.0178 × 10 / 0.78 = 0.228 Ω.
  2. U = R I = 0.228 × 5.0 = 1.14 V.
  3. Check: increasing the wire length or current increases the drop; using Ω mm2/m with area in mm2 gives Ω.

Trap: keep area units consistent with resistivity. If ρ is in Ω m, put area in m2; if ρ is in Ω mm2/m, put area in mm2.

Near miss: R = ρl/A does not apply unchanged to a varying cross-section or material. Divide the path into pieces or return to the field relation.

Try it in the Chapter 2 lab: 2.2 Uniform wire

Recall check

  1. How does doubling length affect resistance if everything else stays fixed?
  2. What assumptions let the field relation reduce to a single scalar resistance?
Answers
  1. It doubles resistance.
  2. Uniform material and geometry, with uniform current over the cross-section so a single length and area describe the path.

2.2.4 Overview of resistors

A resistor is a physical component designed to provide a useful, specified resistance. Its nominal value and tolerance communicate how closely a manufactured part should match that value.

In plain words: real resistors come in different constructions and sizes. The colour bands or printed code identify a nominal resistance; a tolerance tells you how far the actual value may lie from it.

Allowed range from nominal valueRactual ∈ [Rnom(1 − τ), Rnom(1 + τ)]Read it as: “actual resistance lies within the nominal value plus or minus its fractional tolerance”.τ is a fraction, for example 0.05 for ±5%. Unit: Ω. The notes show E6 ±20%, E12 ±10%, and E24 ±5% preferred-value series.

The lecture shows layer resistors, wire-wound resistors, and examples of fixed resistors. Construction affects practical properties, but the circuit model uses the component’s resistance. The E6, E12, and E24 series offer progressively more nominal values per decade; they are selected-value series, not extra physical laws.

Worked example

A fixed resistor is labelled 1.8 kΩ ±5%. Find its allowed range.

  1. Convert the tolerance to a fraction: τ = 0.05.
  2. Lower limit: 1.8 kΩ × 0.95 = 1.71 kΩ. Upper limit: 1.8 kΩ × 1.05 = 1.89 kΩ.
  3. Check: the range is centred on 1.8 kΩ and extends 0.09 kΩ either way.

Trap: tolerance is a range around a nominal value; it is not an extra resistance to add in series.

Near miss: a 1 kΩ ±10% part is not guaranteed to equal exactly 1.0 kΩ. Its permitted range is 0.90–1.10 kΩ.

Try it in the Chapter 2 lab: 2.2 Resistor tolerance

Recall check

  1. What does ±5% mean for a 200 Ω nominal resistor?
  2. What is the distinction between a preferred-value series and a resistor construction?
Answers
  1. The allowed range is 190–210 Ω.
  2. A preferred-value series gives nominal values and tolerances; construction describes how the component is made, such as layer or wire-wound.

2.3 Behaviour at interfaces

At a conductivity boundary, the field and current can change size and direction.

In plain words: split each vector into parts normal to and along the boundary. Charge conservation preserves normal current; the field integral preserves tangential field.

Continuous componentsJn1 = Jn2,   Et1 = Et2Read it as: “normal current density and tangential electric field are continuous across the interface”.n is normal; t is tangential. Subscripts 1 and 2 name the materials.
Components that change with conductivityEn1En2 = κ2κ1,   Jt1Jt2 = κ1κ2Read it as: “the normal electric field and tangential current density change inversely with conductivity”.These ratios follow from local J = κE.
Refraction of the current directiontan α1tan α2 = κ1κ2Read it as: “the tangent of the current angle changes in the ratio of the conductivities”.α is measured from the normal; tan α = Jt/Jn.

At an insulator, no current crosses, so J is tangential on the conducting side. At a perfect conductor, the outside field and current are normal to its surface.

Worked example

For κ1 = 2 S/m, κ2 = 8 S/m, Jn = 4 A/m2 and Et = 3 V/m, find the remaining components.

  1. En1 = 4/2 = 2 V/m; En2 = 4/8 = 0.50 V/m.
  2. Jt1 = 2 × 3 = 6 A/m2; Jt2 = 8 × 3 = 24 A/m2.
  3. Check: Jn and Et match across the boundary.

Trap: keep Jn and Et continuous; use Ohm’s law for the other components.

Near miss: equal κ makes all components continuous; unequal κ guarantees continuity only for the named components.

Try it in the Chapter 2 lab: 2.3 Conductivity interface

Recall check

  1. Which boundary conditions come from charge continuity and which from the field integral?
  2. At an insulating interface, can current have a normal component?
Answers
  1. Jn continuity follows from charge continuity; Et continuity follows from the vanishing loop integral as the loop height shrinks.
  2. No. The normal current must be zero on the insulating side and therefore on the conducting side too.

2.4 Energy and power

Power is energy transfer per time; a resistor converts electrical energy to heat.

In plain words: voltage is energy per charge and current is charge per time, so their product is power.

Electric power in a resistorP = U I = U2R = I2RRead it as: “power is voltage times current, or voltage squared over resistance, or current squared times resistance”.For fixed R, use U = R I. Unit: W = V A = J/s.
Power density and energypV = E · JW = ∫t0t0+T P(t) dtRead it as: “power density is field dotted with current density; energy is power integrated over time”.pV is W/m3 and integrates to power. Energy is in J; 1 kWh = 3.6 × 106 J. At constant power, W = P T.

From J = κE, pV = κ|E|2 = |J|2/κ. For time-varying values, instantaneous power is u(t)i(t); integrate it over time.

Worked example

A 24 Ω resistor runs at 12 V DC for 3.0 minutes. Find current, power, and energy.

  1. I = U/R = 12/24 = 0.50 A.
  2. P = U I = 12 × 0.50 = 6.0 W.
  3. T = 3.0 min = 180 s, so W = P T = 6.0 × 180 = 1080 J.
  4. Check: 6 W × 180 s = 1080 J.

Trap: watts measure power; joules measure energy. A watt-hour is power multiplied by time.

Near miss: the two resistor power forms agree only when U, I, and R obey Ohm’s law.

Try it in the Chapter 2 lab: 2.4 Power and energy

Recall check

  1. How do you obtain energy from a time-varying power?
  2. How many joules are in 1 kWh?
Answers
  1. Integrate instantaneous power over the time interval: W = ∫P(t)dt.
  2. 3.6 × 106 J.

Derive it cold

Rebuild each chain on blank paper, without looking at the formula map.

  1. From charge crossing a section in a time interval, take the limit to define I = dQ/dt. §2.1.1
  2. Count the signed charge in a thin slab moving at velocity v; divide by area and time to obtain J = ϱVv. §2.1.2
  3. Use electron charge density and drift velocity to obtain κ = neeμe and J = κE. §2.2.1–2.2.2
  4. For a uniform cylindrical wire, substitute J = I/A into E = J/κ, integrate over length, and identify R = ρRl/A. §2.2.3
  5. Use a shrinking pillbox and a shrinking loop at a material boundary to derive continuity of Jn and Et. §2.3
  6. Start with work per charge and charge per time to derive P = U I; substitute Ohm’s law for the two resistor forms. §2.4
  7. Integrate the instantaneous rate of energy transfer to get W = ∫P(t)dt; reduce it to P T for constant power. §2.4

Explain it

Answer each in two or three sentences without writing a formula first.

  1. Why can a wire carry a stationary current while its charge carriers keep moving?
  2. Why does electron drift point opposite conventional current in a metal?
  3. Why does doubling a wire’s diameter reduce its resistance by a factor of four, if length and material stay fixed?
  4. Why are only the normal current density and tangential field guaranteed continuous across a conductivity boundary?
  5. When does a resistor’s power increase if its resistance increases: at fixed voltage or at fixed current?
  6. Why is a current-density field more informative than a single current value for a spatially varying conductor?
Model answers
  1. Stationary means the flow at each point does not change with time; it does not mean the carriers are at rest. Charge entering each closed region is balanced by charge leaving it.
  2. Electrons carry negative charge, so the electric force on them is opposite the field. Conventional current is defined in the direction positive charge would travel.
  3. Area is proportional to diameter squared. Since resistance is inversely proportional to area, four times the area gives one quarter the resistance.
  4. Charge conservation through a thin pillbox preserves normal current; the vanishing electric-field integral around a shrinking loop preserves the tangential field. Local Ohm’s law then determines the other components.
  5. At fixed current, P = I2R rises with resistance. At fixed voltage, P = U2/R falls with resistance.
  6. A single total hides how current is distributed and in which direction it crosses each surface. The vector field gives both local direction and density, so it can be integrated over any chosen surface.

Exam-style problems

Allow about 20 minutes per problem. Attempt each part in order; later parts use earlier results.

1 · A copper lead

A uniform copper lead has length 8.0 m, diameter 2.0 mm, and conductivity 56 × 106 S/m at its operating temperature. It carries 1.5 A.

  1. Find its cross-sectional area and current density.
  2. Find the electric field in the lead and state its direction relative to conventional current.
  3. Find its resistance and voltage drop.
  4. Find the power dissipated and the energy converted in 10 minutes.
Worked answer
  1. Radius = 1.0 × 10−3 m, so A = πr2 = 3.14 × 10−6 m2. J = I/A = 4.77 × 105 A/m2. §2.1.2, §2.2.1
  2. E = J/κ = 8.53 × 10−3 V/m. It points with conventional current. §2.1.2, §2.2.2
  3. R = l/(κA) = 0.0455 Ω; U = R I = 0.0682 V. §2.2.2, §2.2.3
  4. P = I2R = 0.102 W. For 600 s, W = P T = 61.4 J. Check U I gives the same power. §2.2.3, §2.4

2 · Current crossing a material boundary

Two conducting materials meet at a flat interface. Their conductivities are κ1 = 2 S/m and κ2 = 8 S/m. In material 1, Jn1 = 4 A/m2 and Et1 = 3 V/m. A uniform region of volume 0.010 m3 in material 2 carries these local fields.

  1. Find Jn2, En1, and En2.
  2. Find Et2 and the two tangential current densities.
  3. Find the current-density direction angle in each material relative to the normal, then compare the angles.
  4. Find the power density and total power dissipated in the stated volume in material 2.
Worked answer
  1. Normal current is continuous: Jn2 = 4 A/m2. Local Ohm’s law gives En1 = 4/2 = 2 V/m and En2 = 4/8 = 0.50 V/m. §2.2.2, §2.3
  2. Tangential field is continuous, so Et2 = 3 V/m. Then Jt1 = 2 × 3 = 6 A/m2 and Jt2 = 8 × 3 = 24 A/m2. §2.2.2, §2.3
  3. tan α1 = 6/4 = 1.5, so α1 ≈ 56.3°. tan α2 = 24/4 = 6, so α2 ≈ 80.5°. Thus tan α1/tan α2 = 1/4 = κ1/κ2, as required. §2.3
  4. pV2 = En2Jn2 + Et2Jt2 = 0.5 × 4 + 3 × 24 = 74 W/m3. Total P = 74 × 0.010 = 0.74 W. §2.2.2, §2.3, §2.4
