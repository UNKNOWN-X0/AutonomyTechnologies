Task 9: Electrostatics

Two ideal conducting coaxial cylinders have radii a and b (with 0 < a < b) and length l. The annular gap contains permittivity ε1 for 90° < φ < 270° and ε2 for −90° < φ < 90°. These are absolute permittivities. The conductors are held at potentials φe,1 on the outer cylinder and φe,0 on the inner; U10 = φe,1 − φe,0. Neglect fringing at the finite ends.

Connected topics: Dielectric interfaces (§1.11) supplies the field boundary conditions; capacitance (§1.13) connects the resulting charge and voltage.

9a: Sketch the electric field

Try first: identify the direction perpendicular to the conducting cylinders and compare it with the normal to each dielectric interface.

Attempted it? Open the worked steps

9a · Qualitative field

  1. The metal cylinders are equipotentials. Away from the finite ends, the field meets each cylindrical conductor normally, so its direction is radial, along eρ.

  2. The voltage is U10 = φe,outer − φe,inner. If U10 > 0, the outer conductor is at higher potential, so E points inward (−eρ) from the outer cylinder toward the inner one. Reverse the arrows if U10 < 0.

  3. Draw radial lines from radius b to radius a, with denser spacing near the inner cylinder because the field magnitude varies as 1/ρ. Neglect end fringing as the ideal long-coax model requires.

9b: Behaviour at the dielectric interface

Try first: at φ = ±90°, name the surface normal and resolve the radial field into normal and tangential components.

Attempted it? Open the worked steps

9b · Boundary conditions

  1. A constant-φ interface has normal n = ±eφ. The radial field is perpendicular to that normal, so it lies tangent to the interface:

    En,1 = En,2 = 0,    Et,1 = Et,2

  2. With no free charge on the dielectric boundary, the normal component of displacement is continuous; it is zero on both sides here. Tangential displacement need not be continuous and scales with each material permittivity:

    Dt,1Dt,2 = ε1ε2

  3. Thus E crosses the angular boundary without a change in direction or magnitude. D changes magnitude at the boundary in proportion to ε. The radial field lines run along the interface, rather than crossing it.

9c: Mathematical approach for E

Try first: write Laplace’s equation for a potential independent of φ and z, then use the two conductor potentials as boundary values.

Attempted it? Open the worked steps

9c · Radial solution

  1. The coaxial geometry and constant conductor potentials allow a z- and φ-independent potential in each sector. The cylindrical Laplace equation reduces to:

    1ρ · ddρ(ρ dφedρ) = 0

    φe(ρ) = A ln ρ + B

  2. Apply φe(a) = φe,0 and φe(b) = φe,1. The same solution works in both sectors because their conductor boundary values are identical and the radial field is tangent to the interface:

    φe(ρ) = φe,0 + U10ln(b/a) ln(ρa)

  3. The electric field is the negative potential gradient:

    E(ρ) = −U10ρ ln(b/a) eρ

    The 1/ρ dependence is required by cylindrical symmetry. Positive U10 gives the inward direction.

9d · Piecewise permittivity

  1. For a linear, isotropic dielectric, D = εE. The permittivity depends on which half-annulus contains the point:

    D(ρ, φ) = ε1 E(ρ)     for 90° < φ < 270°

    D(ρ, φ) = ε2 E(ρ)     for −90° < φ < 90°

  2. Because E is the same on both sides of the radial interface, the displacement magnitudes differ by ε1/ε2. At φ = ±90° the normal displacement remains zero and continuous.

9e: Sign of charge on the outer cylinder

Try first: compare the specified potential of the outer conductor with the inner one and use the direction of E.

Attempted it? Open the worked steps

9e · Outer-conductor charge sign

  1. For U10 > 0, the outer cylinder’s potential exceeds the inner cylinder’s. The field points from the outer conductor toward the inner conductor.

  2. The normal from the metal into the dielectric at the outer cylinder’s inner wall is −eρ, parallel to E. The conductor surface charge density there is D · (−eρ) > 0. Therefore the net charge on the outer cylinder is positive.

9f: Find E as a function of outer charge Q

Try first: relate the field scale to the charge on the outer conductor by integrating D over its inner cylindrical surface, split into two half-circumferences.

Attempted it? Open the worked steps

9f · Charge fixes the field scale

  1. Let the inward field have magnitude K/ρ, so D in sector i has magnitude εiK/ρ. The normal from the outer metal into the gap is −eρ; its facing area is half the cylinder, πb l, in each sector:

    Q = ε1 Kb(πbl) + ε2 Kb(πbl)

  2. Solve for the field constant and substitute into the radial field:

    K = Qπl(ε1 + ε2)

    E(ρ) = −Qπl(ε1 + ε2) ρ eρ

    This is uniform in φ although D is piecewise. The sign of Q sets the field direction.

9g: Find U10 as a function of Q

Try first: integrate the inward radial field between a and b, keeping the voltage reference U10 = φe,outer − φe,inner straight.

Attempted it? Open the worked steps

9g · Charge–voltage relation

  1. Because the field points inward, the outer-minus-inner voltage is U10 = −∫ab E · eρ dρ:

    U10 = K ∫ab dρρ = K ln(b/a)

  2. Insert K from 9f:

    U10 = Q ln(b/a)πl(ε1 + ε2)

    Positive Q gives positive U10, consistent with 9e.

9h: Capacitance and numerical value

Try first: use C = Q/U10, then substitute the two sector permittivities and dimensions.

Attempted it? Open the worked steps

9h · Coaxial capacitance

  1. For this linear arrangement, capacitance is the charge magnitude per applied voltage. Cancel Q using 9g:

    C = πl(ε1 + ε2)ln(b/a)

  2. With ε1 = ε0, ε2 = 3ε0, a = 0.020 m, b = 0.021 m and l = 0.10 m:

    C = 4πε0 · 0.10 mln(0.021/0.020)

    C ≈ 2.28 × 10−10 F = 228 pF

    Check: a uniform ε0 gap would have half this capacitance, since the mean permittivity here is 2ε0. The result is also positive and has units of farads.
