Electrostatic Fields

Electrical Engineering I · Module 92315 · Chapter 1

Charges at rest create a field. The field pushes on other charges, stores energy, and changes when matter fills the space. This chapter follows that chain from a single point charge to the capacitor.

Start from memory

Before reading, answer these six prompts without notes. A wrong guess is useful: it tells you what to look for while you read.

  1. What changes when you measure the field E instead of the force F?
  2. Why can the fields of several charges simply be added?
  3. What does Gauss’s law count when you integrate over a closed surface?
  4. Why is the inside of a metal body field-free in electrostatics?
  5. At a boundary between two insulators with no surface charge, which stays continuous: the normal part of D or the normal part of E?
  6. What is the difference between a capacitor’s capacitance and its stored energy?
Check the short answers
  1. E is a property of the space set up by the source charges; it does not depend on the probe. The force depends on the probe: F = Q E.
  2. Experiments show that the forces from several charges on a probe add as vectors. In a homogeneous medium the fields therefore add as vectors too (superposition).
  3. The total charge inside the surface: ∮ D · dA = Qenclosed.
  4. Free charges move under any field inside the metal until their own field cancels it. In the static state nothing moves any more, so the interior field is zero.
  5. The normal part of D. The normal part of E jumps in the ratio ε2/ε1.
  6. Capacitance C = Q/U depends only on geometry and material. Stored energy ½C U2 also depends on how far the capacitor is charged.

1.1 Electric charge

The observation. Rub two glass rods with wool and they repel; two plastic rods repel too; a glass rod and a plastic rod attract. Mechanics cannot explain this, so a new property is introduced: electric charge. Because there is both attraction and repulsion, there must be two kinds. By convention glass gets +, plastic gets −.

Unit and elementary charge1 C = 1 A s;   e ≈ 1.602 × 10−19 C;   proton +e, electron −e, neutron 0Every charge is a whole multiple of e. A neutral atom has as many protons as electrons.
  • Charge is separated, not created. Rubbing glass moves electrons onto the wool, leaving the glass with an electron deficit (+). Plastic takes electrons from the wool and ends up with an excess (−).
  • Conservation: the total charge of a closed system is constant. Charges only appear or disappear in equal and opposite pairs.

Recall check

  1. Why does the rubbing experiment force us to introduce exactly two kinds of charge?
  2. A body carries −4.8 × 10−19 C. What happened to it at the level of electrons?
Answers
  1. We see both attraction and repulsion; one kind of charge could only produce one of them.
  2. −4.8 × 10−19/1.602 × 10−19 ≈ −3: it gained three electrons.

1.2 Coulomb’s law

For two point charges (bodies much smaller than their distance), the force grows with each charge and falls with the square of the distance. The same 1/r2 appears in Newton’s gravitation; the 4πr2 in the denominator is the area of a sphere of radius r.

Force on charge 2 caused by charge 1F2 = 14π ε0 · Q1Q2r2 er,   er = rP2 − rP1rε0 = 8.854 × 10−12 A s/(V m); 1/(4π ε0) ≈ 8.988 × 109 V m/(A s). er points from the charge that causes the force to the charge that feels it.
  • Signs are built in. If Q1Q2 > 0 the force points along er (repulsion); if < 0 it points against it (attraction). Put the signed charges in and the direction comes out right.
  • Action = reaction: the force on charge 1 has the same size and the opposite direction.

Worked example

Q1 = +4 μC sits at the origin, Q2 = −1 μC at (0, 0.3 m). Find the force on Q2.

  1. er points from Q1 to Q2: er = +ey, r = 0.3 m.
  2. Size: 8.988 × 109 × (4 × 10−6)(1 × 10−6)/0.09 ≈ 0.40 N.
  3. The product Q1Q2 is negative, so F2 ≈ −0.40 N ey: toward Q1, an attraction, as opposite signs should give.

Trap: r is the distance between the charges, not a position coordinate. Point charges are an idealisation; Coulomb’s law in this form needs bodies much smaller than their distance.

Recall check

  1. Which way does er point in Coulomb’s law?
  2. Both charges are tripled and the distance is tripled. What happens to the force?
Answers
  1. From the charge that produces the force toward the charge on which it acts.
  2. Nothing: 3 × 3/32 = 1.

1.3 Electric field strength

Coulomb’s law describes a pair of charges but says nothing about the space around them. The field idea fixes that: a charge changes the surrounding space, and that change is felt by any probe charge placed there. A field assigns a physical quantity to every point in space; temperature is a scalar field, gravity and E are vector fields.

Field of one point charge Q at observation point PE(rP) = Q4π ε0r2 er = Q4π ε0 · rr3,   r = rP − rQForce on a probe: F = Qprobe E. Unit of E: V/m (equivalent to N/C).
  • E does not depend on the probe: a bigger probe charge feels a bigger force, but F/Q stays the same.
  • The field exists in vacuum; no matter is needed.
  • A positive probe is pushed along E, a negative probe against it.
  • The vector r always runs from the source point Q to the observation point P.

Recall check

  1. The probe charge is doubled. What happens to E and to F?
  2. Write the field of a point charge in the r/r3 form and say what r is.
Answers
  1. E is unchanged; F doubles.
  2. E = Q r/(4π ε0 r3), where r = rP − rQ points from source to observation point.

1.4 Superposition of fields

Experiments show that the forces of several charges on one probe add as vectors. In a homogeneous medium the total field is therefore the vector sum of the individual fields.

Field of N point chargesE(rP) = 14π ε0 Σi=1N Qi riri3,   ri = rP − rQiThe force on a further charge Q4 at P is Q4 E(rP), where the sum leaves out Q4 itself: a charge does not push on itself.
  1. Sketch the charges and P with coordinates.
  2. For each charge write ri = rP − rQi and its length.
  3. Write each field with the signed charge.
  4. Add x, y and z components separately; look for components that cancel by symmetry.

Worked example

Two charges of +2 nC sit at (0, +3 cm) and (0, −3 cm). Find E at P = (4 cm, 0).

  1. r1 = (0.04, −0.03) m and r2 = (0.04, +0.03) m, both of length 0.05 m.
  2. Each field has size 8.988 × 109 × 2 × 10−9/0.052 ≈ 7.19 × 103 V/m along its own ri/ri.
  3. The y-components (∓0.6 of each) cancel; the x-components (0.8 of each) add: E ≈ 2 × 0.8 × 7.19 × 103 ≈ 1.15 × 104 V/m along +x.

Trap: adding magnitudes. Two 7.19 kV/m fields gave 11.5 kV/m here, not 14.4 kV/m, because they point in different directions.

Recall check

  1. Under what condition does the notes’ superposition formula hold?
  2. Why does the field of the other charges, not the total field, act on a charge in the arrangement?
Answers
  1. In a homogeneous (linear) medium; that is where the fields of the individual charges simply add.
  2. A charge exerts no net force on itself, so only the field of the other charges at its position counts.

1.5 Forces between charge distributions

The notes only pose two questions here, using two charged bodies V1 and V2. Both follow from what you already know:

  1. Forces inside one body. Every pair of charges inside the body pushes or pulls on each other with equal and opposite forces, so these internal forces add to zero for the body as a whole. They still matter: they decide where charge settles. In a conductor, for example, excess charge spreads out to the surface.
  2. Forces between two bodies. Cut V2 into small elements dQ = ϱ dV, find the field E1 that V1 alone produces at each element, and add up dF = E1 dQ. The force on V1 is equal and opposite.
Force on body 2 from body 1F2 = ∭V2 ϱ2 E1 dVThis applies F = Q E element by element. The hard part is finding E1, which is what the rest of the chapter is about.

1.6 Charge densities

A point charge is an idealised charge squeezed into a single point, so its density would be infinite. Real charge is spread out, and you describe it with a density defined as a limit: take a small piece of line, area or volume around a point, divide its charge by its size, and let the piece shrink onto the point.

DistributionDensityUniform caseTotal chargeUnit
Lineλ = dQ/dlλ = Q/lQ = ∫l λ dlC/m

A surface charge is idealised as infinitely thin. Pick the volume element that matches the shape (appendix 7.3 of the notes): dV = dx dy dz (Cartesian), ρ dρ dφ dz (cylinder), r2 sin ϑ dr dϑ dφ (sphere). If ϱ depends only on r, use thin shells: dV = 4πr2 dr.

Worked example

A sphere of radius R has ϱ(r) = ϱ0(1 − r/R). Find its total charge.

  1. The density depends only on r, so use shells: Q = ∫0R ϱ0(1 − r/R) 4πr2 dr.
  2. = 4πϱ0 (R3/3 − R3/4) = πϱ0R3/3.
  3. Check: a uniform ϱ0 would give 4πϱ0R3/3. Our density falls to zero at the edge, so a quarter of that is plausible.

Recall check

  1. Give the three density symbols with their units.
  2. When can you write Q = σAA, and what must you do otherwise?
Answers
  1. λ in C/m, σA in C/m², ϱV in C/m³.
  2. Only for a uniform distribution. Otherwise keep the density inside the integral and integrate over the surface.

1.7 Field lines

A field line is a curve whose tangent at every point has the direction of E. Arrows on the line show the direction. In vector plots the arrow length shows the field strength; in line plots the density of lines does.

  1. Construction: find E at a starting point.
  2. Step a short distance in that direction and find E again.
  3. Repeat. The field line is the curve that is tangent to all these vectors.
  • Lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges (or at infinity).
  • Lines meet conductor surfaces at right angles (shown in 1.8 and 1.11).
  • Lines never cross where E ≠ 0: a point cannot have two field directions. They can only meet at field-free points.

Patterns to sketch from memory: a positive point charge (radial, outward), a negative one (radial, inward), the ideal plate capacitor (parallel lines from + to −), a + and − pair (lines bend from one charge into the other), and two equal + charges (lines push apart, with a field-free point exactly between them).

Uniqueness theorem: a field that satisfies Maxwell’s equations and the boundary conditions is the only possible solution. Two arrangements with the same boundary conditions therefore have the same field. This is the deeper reason field lines cannot cross.

Recall check

  1. Why is the midpoint between two equal positive charges field-free, and why can field lines meet only there?
  2. State four rules for reading a field-line diagram.
Answers
  1. The two fields there are equal in size and opposite in direction. Lines may only meet where E = 0, because anywhere else the direction is unique.
  2. The tangent gives the direction of E; lines run from + to −; denser lines mean a stronger field; lines never cross where E ≠ 0; lines meet conductors at right angles.

1.8 Electrostatic potential

Work. Moving a charge Q from P0 to P1 against the field costs energy; moving it with the field releases energy. The notes count the work supplied from outside, hence the minus sign.

Externally supplied workWe = −∫P0P1 F · ds = −Q ∫P0P1 E · dsWe > 0: energy is supplied to the system. We < 0: the field does the work and the system gives energy away.

Path independence. Carry the charge from P0 along one path to P1 and back along another. Afterwards the field is exactly as before, so the system’s energy is unchanged and the total work is zero. Since the return path was arbitrary, the work from P0 to P1 cannot depend on the path.

The electrostatic field is conservative∮C E · ds = 0   and therefore   ∮C F · ds = 0The notes call it a source field, like gravity: the charges are its sources.

Potential. Because the work depends only on the end points, every point P can be given a number: the work per unit charge needed to bring a charge there from a reference point P0, where φe(P0) = 0.

Potential (unit V)φe(P) = We(P)Q = −∫P0P E · ds,   We = Q(φe(Pend) − φe(Pstart))Usual references: earth, infinity, or circuit ground (the negative battery pole or a metal housing). Like height above sea level, only differences are physical.
Point charge Q0, reference at infinityWe = Q Q04π ε0 (1r2 − 1r1)   (from r1 to r2),   φe(r) = Q04π ε0rPotential is a scalar: for several charges add the signed potentials, no components needed. E ∝ 1/r2, D ∝ 1/r2, φ ∝ 1/r.
Move Q …same signs (Q Q0 > 0)opposite signs (Q Q0 < 0)
toward Q0 (r2 < r1)We > 0: you push against repulsionWe < 0: attraction does the work
away from Q0 (r2 > r1)We < 0: repulsion does the workWe > 0: you pull against attraction

Equipotential surfaces

An equipotential surface collects all points of equal potential; in a drawing its cuts appear as equipotential lines. Moving along one costs no work, so E · ds = 0 there: equipotentials cross field lines at right angles. Drawn at equal potential steps, closely spaced equipotentials mean a strong field.

Conductors. A tangential field at a metal surface would push the free charges along it, and they would keep moving until that component vanished. In the static state the whole conductor therefore sits at one potential, its surface is an equipotential, field lines meet it at right angles, and the interior is field-free.

Worked example

How much work is needed to bring +1 nC from infinity to 0.10 m from a fixed +2 nC?

  1. φ at 0.10 m: 8.988 × 109 × 2 × 10−9/0.10 ≈ 180 V; φ(∞) = 0.
  2. We = Q(φend − φstart) = 10−9 × 180 ≈ 1.8 × 10−7 J.
  3. Positive, as the table predicts for pushing like charges together.

Recall check

  1. Explain in two sentences why ∮ E · ds = 0 in electrostatics.
  2. A negative charge is moved away from a positive charge. Is We positive or negative?
  3. Why are equipotentials perpendicular to field lines?
Answers
  1. After a round trip the field and the system’s energy are exactly as before, so the net work is zero. Because this holds for any loop, the work between two points cannot depend on the path.
  2. Positive: you pull against the attraction.
  3. Moving along an equipotential costs no work, so E · ds = 0 for every step along it; E must be perpendicular.

1.9 Voltage

Write φe(P1) − φe(P2) with the definition of potential. The two integrals from the reference point P0 join into one integral from P1 to P2, and P0 drops out: a potential difference does not depend on the reference point.

Voltage from P1 to P2 (unit V)U12 = φe(P1) − φe(P2) = ∫P1P2 E · dsAny path from P1 to P2 gives the same value. Uniform field along a path of length d: U = E d.
  • U12 > 0 means P1 is at the higher potential. E points from high to low potential.
  • Link to work: moving Q from P1 to P2 needs We = Q(φ2 − φ1) = −Q U12.

Sign check: U12 is “first point minus second point”: φ1 − φ2. Read the subscripts in that order every time.

Recall check

  1. Show in one line why the reference point P0 drops out of U12.
  2. φA = 5 V and φB = 8 V. What is UAB, and which way does E roughly point?
Answers
  1. φ1 − φ2 = −∫P0P1 + ∫P0P2 = ∫P1P0 + ∫P0P2 = ∫P1P2 E · ds.
  2. UAB = −3 V; E points from B (higher) toward A.

1.10 Electric flux density and Gauss’s law

Two field quantities. E describes the effect (the force on charges). The flux density D describes the cause and makes the influence of matter explicit. For the linear materials in this course they are proportional.

Flux density (unit A s/m² = C/m²)D = ε E = ε0 εr Eεr = 1 in vacuum and, to a good approximation, in air.
Electric flux through a surface (unit A s = C)Ψ = ∬A D · dA,   dA = n dAn is the unit normal of the surface element. Only the part of D along n contributes.

Language trap: “flux” and “flux density” are historical names. Nothing flows in an electrostatic field; do not confuse flux with current, where charges really move.

Gauss’s law

Around a point charge in vacuum, D = Q/(4πr2) er. On a concentric sphere D is parallel to dA and has constant size, so the flux is D × 4πr2 = Q, whatever the radius. The same holds for any closed surface and any position of the charge inside it, and by superposition for any charge distribution:

Gauss’s law in integral formΨ = ∯A D · dA = Qenclosed = ∭V ϱV dVValid always, not only in electrostatics. In this course the material is described by ε, so Q means the charges you place (on electrodes or given as densities). The material’s own polarisation charges are already included through ε.

Gauss’s law always holds, but it only gives you the field when symmetry tells you the direction of D and that its size is constant on the surface. In practice that means three shapes: sphere (point charge, charged shell or ball), infinitely long cylinder (line charge, coax) and infinite plane (plate capacitor).

  1. From the symmetry, state the direction of D and which coordinate it can depend on.
  2. Choose a closed surface on which D is either perpendicular and constant, or parallel to the surface (zero flux).
  3. Find the enclosed charge as a function of the surface size.
  4. Set flux = enclosed charge, solve for D, then E = D/ε.

Worked example from the notes: long uniformly charged cylinder

Radius a, uniform ϱV. Use a coaxial cylinder of radius ρ and length l.

  1. By symmetry D = Dρ(ρ) eρ. On the end caps eρ · (±ez) = 0, so only the curved side counts: flux = Dρ 2πρl.
  2. Enclosed charge: ϱV π ρ2 l inside (ρ < a), ϱV π a2 l outside (ρ ≥ a).
  3. Dρ = ϱV ρ/2 inside and ϱV a2/(2ρ) outside: it rises linearly to ϱVa/2 at the surface, then falls as 1/ρ. E = D/ε.

Worked example: the same charge in a dielectric

A 1 nC point charge sits in oil with εr = 2.5. At r = 0.10 m, D = 10−9/(4π × 0.01) ≈ 7.96 × 10−9 C/m², exactly as in vacuum, because Gauss’s law only sees the charge. But E = D/(2.5 ε0) ≈ 360 V/m instead of 899 V/m.

Recall check

  1. State Gauss’s law in words, including the direction in which the flux is counted.
  2. Why do the end caps contribute nothing in the cylinder example?
  3. A charge is moved from vacuum into a dielectric. Which of D and E changes?
Answers
  1. The outward flux of D through any closed surface equals the total charge enclosed.
  2. D is radial and the cap normals point along ±z, so D · dA = 0 there.
  3. D stays the same (set by the charge); E = D/(ε0εr) drops by a factor of εr.

1.11 Interface conditions

At a boundary, split E and D into a normal part (along the unit normal n) and a tangential part (along a unit tangent t). Each part is fixed by one law:

  1. Normal part from Gauss’s law. Take a flat pillbox that straddles the boundary and shrink its height h → 0. Only the two faces contribute: (Dn2 − Dn1)A = enclosed charge = σAA.
  2. Tangential part from ∮ E · ds = 0. Take a thin rectangular loop across the boundary and shrink its width h → 0. Only the two long sides of length l contribute: (Et2 − Et1)l = 0.
  3. Get the remaining parts from D = εE on each side.
BoundaryNormal partTangential part
Two dielectrics, no surface chargeDn1 = Dn2, so En1/En2 = ε2/ε1Et1 = Et2, so Dt1/Dt2 = ε1/ε2
Surface charge σA in vacuumDn2 − Dn1 = σA, with n pointing from side 1 into side 2Et1 = Et2 and Dt1 = Dt2
Conductor (1) to vacuum (2)Inside D = E = 0; outside D = σAn, E = (σA/ε0)n, n pointing out of the metalEt = Dt = 0

Refraction of field lines. The two parts change differently, so a field line bends at the boundary, much like light. Measuring the angle α from the normal, tan α = Et/En, and the two conditions give tan α1/tan α2 = ε1/ε2: in the medium with the larger ε the line leans further from the normal. (This rule follows directly from the table; the notes only mention the analogy with light.)

Worked example

A field of 100 V/m in vacuum hits a dielectric with εr = 4 at 45° to the normal. There is no surface charge.

  1. Split: En1 = Et1 = 100 cos 45° ≈ 70.7 V/m.
  2. Tangential continuous: Et2 = 70.7 V/m. Normal D continuous: En2 = 70.7/4 ≈ 17.7 V/m.
  3. E2 = √(70.7² + 17.7²) ≈ 72.9 V/m at arctan(70.7/17.7) ≈ 76° from the normal. Check: tan 45°/tan 76° = 1/4 = ε1/ε2.

Ideal plate capacitor from two charged sheets

  1. One infinite sheet with +σA: by symmetry D has the same size on both sides and points away from the sheet. The jump condition gives D − (−D) = σA, so D = σA/2 on each side.
  2. The −σA sheet gives σA/2 pointing toward it on both sides.
  3. Superpose: outside the plates the two contributions cancel; between them they add to D = σA, directed from + to −.
Ideal plate capacitor (fringing neglected)D = σA,   E = σAε,   U = E dReal plates have curved fringing fields at their edges. This course always neglects them.

Recall check

  1. Which closed surface and which closed path do you use to derive the two interface conditions, and why must h → 0?
  2. Why is Et = 0 just outside a conductor?
  3. Field lines pass from a medium with εr = 2 into one with εr = 8. Do they bend toward or away from the normal?
Answers
  1. A thin pillbox (Gauss) for the normal part and a thin rectangle (∮ E · ds = 0) for the tangential part. With h → 0 the side walls contribute nothing, leaving only the two faces or the two long sides.
  2. A tangential field would move the free charges along the surface; in the static state they have already moved until it vanished.
  3. Away from the normal: tan α grows by the factor 8/2 = 4.

1.12 Matter in an electrostatic field

The notes compare three versions of the same ideal plate capacitor. The plates carry a fixed charge ±Q (the capacitor is isolated), so D = σA stays the same throughout. Only E, and therefore U, changes.

Gap filled withField in the slabVoltage
nothing (gap d)—U = E d, the largest
a dielectric slab (relative permittivity εr)E/εrin between
a metal slab of the same thickness0E(d1 + d2), the smallest; only the air gaps count

Memory hook: empty gap > dielectric > metal. The notes label these cases (b) and (c) differently on two pages (see Slips in the notes), so learn the order by material, not by letter.

Conductors: electrostatic induction

Put an uncharged metal body into a field. Its free electrons drift toward the positive plate, leaving a deficit on the far side. The body as a whole stays neutral, but its surfaces now carry opposite charges. If the body is made of two touching sheets that you pull apart while they are still in the field, each sheet keeps its charge. The charges stop moving once the whole body is one equipotential and the inside is field-free. Because these induced charges depend on the field they create, general field problems with conductors are hard to compute.

Hollow conductor. Put a charge Q at the centre of a metal shell with inner radius a and outer radius b. The inner surface collects −Q, the outer surface +Q. D = Q/(4πr2) in the cavity, it drops to zero inside the metal, and outside it looks like the field of the point charge again. At each surface the induced σ equals the D that arrives there, so D jumps by exactly σ, as the interface condition demands.

Dielectrics: polarisation

An insulator has no free charge carriers, but its bound charges can shift or turn:

  • Displacement polarisation: the field pulls an atom’s electron cloud and nucleus slightly apart (electronic), or shifts ions in a crystal (ionic).
  • Orientation polarisation: molecules with a built-in dipole, such as water, turn toward the field.
Dipole moment and polarisationp = Q d   (d from − to +),   P = 1V Σn=1N pnIn a uniform field the two forces on a dipole are equal and opposite: no net force, but a torque that turns p toward E.
How polarisation enters DD = ε0E + P,   P = ε0χE,   χ = εr − 1,   so D = ε0εrEχ is the electric susceptibility; vacuum and air: εr = 1, χ = 0.

The aligned dipoles leave a thin layer of bound charge on each face of the slab, opposite in sign to the neighbouring plate. These polarisation charges weaken the field inside. Read εr two ways: at the same D, E in the dielectric is εr times smaller; at the same E, D is εr times larger.

Worked example

An isolated plate capacitor has voltage Ua = E d with an empty gap. A slab with εr = 4 now fills half the gap. Find the new voltage.

  1. The charge is fixed, so D and the field E in the air stay the same; in the slab the field is E/4.
  2. U = E(d/2) + (E/4)(d/2) = (5/8)E d = 0.625 Ua.
  3. A metal slab of the same thickness would give 0.5 Ua: lower still, as the hook predicts.

Recall check

  1. Order the three voltages (empty, dielectric, metal slab) for a capacitor with fixed charge, and justify the order in one sentence each.
  2. What is the difference between induction and polarisation?
  3. Which way does the dipole moment p point, and what does a uniform field do to a dipole?
Answers
  1. Empty is largest: the full gap has field E. Dielectric is in between: the field in the slab is reduced to E/εr. Metal is smallest: the field in the slab is zero.
  2. Induction moves free charges across a conductor; polarisation only shifts or turns bound charges inside an insulator.
  3. From the negative to the positive charge. A uniform field exerts no net force, only a torque that aligns p with E.

1.13 Capacitors and capacitance

A capacitor is two conductors carrying equal and opposite charge, so that all the flux leaving the positive one ends on the negative one. Scale the charge by any factor and D, E and U scale by the same factor, so Q ∝ U.

Capacitance (unit A s/V = F)C = QU = ∯A D · dA∫s E · dsThe surface encloses one electrode; the path runs from one electrode to the other. C depends only on geometry and material.

Recipe for any geometry: assume ±Q → find D with Gauss → E = D/ε → U = ∫ E · ds → C = Q/U. The Q must cancel; if it does not, there is a mistake.

GeometryD between electrodesCapacitance
Plates, area A, gap dQ/AC = ε0εrA/d
Spherical, radii a < b, vacuumQ/(4πr2)C = 4π ε0 a b/(b − a)
Coaxial, radii a < b, length lQ/(2πρl)C/l = 2π ε/ln(b/a)
  • Spherical: U = Q/(4π ε0) (1/a − 1/b) = Q(b − a)/(4π ε0a b). Limit check: for a thin gap d = b − a ≪ a, C ≈ ε04πa2/d, the plate formula with the sphere’s area.
  • Coaxial: U = Q/(2π ε l) ∫ab dρ/ρ = Q ln(b/a)/(2π ε l).
  • Large C in practice: small gap (but the risk of breakdown grows), large area (metal foil on both sides of a thin film, rolled up: a wound capacitor), or high εr (electrolytic capacitor).

Quick checks: a parallel total is larger than the largest capacitor; a series total is smaller than the smallest. In series, the smallest capacitor takes the largest voltage. These are the reverse of the resistor rules.

Worked example

6 μF and 3 μF in series across 12 V.

  1. 1/C = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2, so C = 2 μF; Q = 2 μF × 12 V = 24 μC on each.
  2. U6 = 24/6 = 4 V and U3 = 24/3 = 8 V. They add to 12 V, and the smaller capacitor carries more voltage.

Built-up capacitors

  • Multilayer: n interleaved plates per terminal form 2n − 1 equal capacitors in parallel: C = (2n − 1) εA/d.
  • Variable (rotary) capacitor: the overlap of rotor and stator is a ring sector of angle α, area α(ra2 − ri2)/2, so C = (2n − 1) ε0α(ra2 − ri2)/(2d). Turning the rotor changes α and hence C.
  • Stray capacitance: any two conductors form a capacitor. A three-phase overhead line has partial capacitances between every pair of wires and from each wire to earth.

Recall check

  1. Recite the five-step recipe for finding C.
  2. Why do series capacitors carry the same charge?
  3. What does the spherical formula reduce to when the gap is very thin?
Answers
  1. Assume ±Q; Gauss for D; E = D/ε; integrate E for U; C = Q/U.
  2. Only the outer plates are charged from outside. Each inner pair of plates forms an isolated, neutral conductor, which induction splits into −Q and +Q.
  3. The plate capacitor with area 4πa2: C ≈ ε04πa2/d.

1.14 Energy stored in an electric field

Charge a capacitor step by step. When it already holds ±q, its voltage is u = q/C. Carrying a further dq from the negative to the positive plate, against the field, costs dWe = u dq = (q/C) dq. (Lower-case letters mark quantities that change with time.)

Energy of a charged capacitorWe = 1C ∫0Q q dq = Q22C = ½ C U2 = ½ Q UNot Q U: the voltage grows from 0 to U during charging, so on average each bit of charge is lifted through only U/2.

Where is the energy? In the field. For a plate capacitor, ½C U2 = ½(εA/d)(E d)2 = ½εE2 × A d, and A d is the volume of the field region. Dividing by the volume gives an energy density that also holds for non-uniform fields:

Energy density (unit J/m³)we = ½ E · D = ½ ε E2,   We = ∭V we dV

Spring analogy: a spring with constant cs stores ½csX2 = ½FX2/cs. Matching it to ½Q2/C, capacitance plays the part of 1/cs (a soft spring) and voltage the part of force.

Worked example: two routes, one answer

Plates: A = 0.01 m², d = 0.5 mm, εr = 3, U = 50 V.

  1. Circuit route: C = 3 × 8.854 × 10−12 × 0.01/(5 × 10−4) ≈ 531 pF; We = ½ × 531 pF × 502 ≈ 0.66 μJ.
  2. Field route: E = U/d = 105 V/m; we = ½ × 3ε0 × 1010 ≈ 0.133 J/m³; volume 5 × 10−6 m³; We ≈ 0.66 μJ.

Recall check

  1. Derive We = Q2/(2C) in three lines.
  2. Write the energy density two ways and give its unit.
Answers
  1. dW = u dq; u = q/C; ∫0Q (q/C) dq = Q2/(2C).
  2. we = ½E · D = ½εE2, in J/m³.

Explain it

Answer aloud in full sentences, as if teaching someone. If you stall, that is the gap to fix.

  1. Why is the electrostatic field conservative, and what does that let you define?
  2. Why must E be zero inside a conductor and perpendicular to its surface?
  3. Why does a dielectric lower the voltage of an isolated charged capacitor, and why does a metal slab lower it even more?
  4. When does Gauss’s law give you the field, and when is it true but useless?
  5. At an uncharged interface, why is the normal part of D continuous while the normal part of E jumps?
  6. Why is a potential tied to a reference point, while a voltage is not?
  7. Why do capacitors in series share the same charge?
  8. Why is the energy of a capacitor ½Q U and not Q U?
Show model answers
  1. After any round trip the field, and so the system’s energy, is unchanged, so the net work is zero. The work between two points is therefore path-independent, which lets you assign each point a potential.
  2. Free charges move under any field. In the static state they have stopped, so there is no field inside and no tangential component at the surface; only a normal component remains outside.
  3. With the charge fixed, D is fixed. A dielectric reduces E in the slab to E/εr through polarisation charges; a metal reduces it to zero through induced charges. Less field over the same distance means less voltage.
  4. When symmetry fixes the direction of D and makes its size constant on a closed surface (sphere, long cylinder, plane). Otherwise it still gives the total flux, but not the field at a point.
  5. A flat pillbox with no enclosed charge has zero total flux, so the normal D going in equals the normal D coming out. Since E = D/ε and ε differs on the two sides, the normal E must differ.
  6. A potential is work per charge measured from a chosen zero point. In a difference of two potentials, the path from the reference cancels out.
  7. Only the outer plates are connected to the source. Each inner pair of plates is an isolated, neutral conductor, so induction can only split it into −Q and +Q.
  8. While charging, the voltage rises from 0 to U. Each bit of charge is moved through the voltage present at that moment, which averages U/2.
